Abstract
Ensuring consistency in distributed systems, particularly in Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types
(CRDTs), poses significant challenges, especially in environments prone to Byzantine faults. Collaborative editing systems such as peer-to-peer (P2P) platforms exemplify these challenges, where
nodes are vulnerable to malicious behavior and data inconsistencies due to transient disconnections. Designing such data types is non-trivial as subtle choices in the semantics of operations
can lead to unintended divergence. Formal verification provides a principled way to address these
complexities by proving that a CRDT satisfies properties like convergence and commutativity.
Existing work shows how individual CRDTs can be verified using theorem provers.
Our proposal offers a structured approach for CRDT verification by developing a modular
framework in the Athena proof assistant. The framework facilitates the specification of both the
syntax and operational semantics of CRDTs and guides the proof of key properties such as convergence, commutativity, and idempotence. By identifying and encapsulating common patterns
in CRDT design and verification. It is suited to developers who are exploring or prototyping new
CRDT variants and need a precise and checkable way to reason about their correctness. While
the framework does not cover all possible CRDT designs. It takes a modest step toward making
formal verification more approachable in this domain.

1 Introduction
Distributed systems replicate data across nodes to allow performance and availability. However,
keeping all replicas consistent when dealing with concurrency, delays, and network partitions
is a significant challenge. Traditional solutions that enforce strong consistency usually rely on
consensus protocols and global coordination, which can be expensive and unstable under failure
scenarios. As an alternative solution, eventual consistency is proposed. In this case updates may
be applied independently at different replicas and reunified later in the process.
Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types (CRDTs) were defined to formalize this idea. CRDTs are
data structures that allow updating concurrently and can be applied without coordination but
still guarantee that all the replicas eventually reach the same state, as long as they receive the
same set of updates. This property, known as Strong Eventual Consistency (SEC) is achieved
through the design of asynchronous update and merge operations which have to satisfy algebraic
conditions including commutativity, associativity, idempotence, and monotonicity [12].
Designing such data types is not always straightforward. Even small design choices, for instance
how to handle removals in sets, can introduce issues that may conduct to divergence or break the
intended semantics. Formal verification provides a solution to avoid behavioral errors by proving
that a CRDTs satisfy properties like convergence and commutativity.
Prior work has demonstrated how to specify and verify CRDTs in theorem provers like Isabelle/HOL [17], establishing rigorous correctness guarantees for individual CRDT implementations. This thesis explores a structured approach by suggesting a more general purpose framework
in the Athena proof assistant for specifying and verifying multiples types of CRDTs. The goal is
to reduce the effort of building dedicated frameworks to verify each independent implementation
of CRDTs by identifying common patterns in their design and behavior, using these patterns
in reusable components. Our proposed framework aims to support the definition of state-based

1 Introduction
CRDTs and helps express their semantics in a modular way. It also provides the structure to
reason about properties like convergence and idempotence.
The contribution is not a complete or fully automated system but a set of structures that
make it easier to work with CRDTs in Athena. In particular, it is aimed at situations where
developers want to experiment with CRDT variants and need a way to express their behaviors
precisely and check key correctness properties. While this framework is limited in scope and not
intended to cover every possible CRDT design; it offers a modest step toward making formal
verification more practical in this domain.
We validate the framework by specifying and verifying six different CRDTs spanning both
state-based and causal designs: GCounter, GSet, PN-Counter, Two-Phase Set, OR-Set, and
LWW-Register. Verification proceeds by proving that each CRDT’s merge and update operations satisfy five semilattice axioms (commutativity, associativity, idempotence, identity, and
monotonicity). These axioms overall imply Strong Eventual Consistency. We show that our
framework enables modular proof reuse: convergence is proven once at the abstract level and inherited by all CRDT instances. Validation includes demonstrations showing replicas converging
under concurrent updates, causality tests verifying that OR-Set behaves under happened-before
ordering, and tests confirming support to message reordering.
The thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 2 surveys existing CRDT verification approaches
and identifies the gap our framework addresses. Chapter 3 develops the mathematical foundations: join-semilattice theory and abstract CRDT axioms. Chapter 4 presents the core framework
architecture, integrating payload semantics (state-based CRDTs), backbone replication (causal
delivery), and the adapter pattern for instantiation. Chapter 5 showcases six verified CRDTs
built using the framework, demonstrating how adapters connect specifications to theory. Chapter 6 validates the framework through comprehensive metrics, execution traces, and chaos testing.
Chapter 7 evaluates the approach critically, discussing strengths, limitations, and lessons learned.
Finally, Chapter 8 summarizes contributions and outlines future directions.

2.1 Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types and eventual
consistency
To provide context for CRDT verification, we start by reviewing the main ideas related to
Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types (CRDTs) and their role in Eventually Consistent systems.
Replication is a typical strategy for achieving high availability and fault tolerance in distributed
systems, however maintaining the consistent aspect of replicated data can be expensive. Traditional approaches that require strong consistency rely on global consensus which may create
performance conflicts with availability under network partitions [12]. The CAP theorem states
that a distributed system can not guarantee availability, partition tolerance and strong consistency at the same time, thus systems often weaken consistency in favour of availability and
partition tolerance [17].
Shapiro et al. formalised this trade-off by introducing the model of Strong Eventual Consistency (SEC). In the context of a eventually consistent system, updates when executed at one
replica are propagated to other replicas asynchronously, possibly in different orders: once all
updates have been distributed, all replicas must converge to the same state [12]. Under SEC two
properties are required: (1) eventual delivery which means every update that is delivered to one
specific replica eventually is delivered to all replicas and (2) convergence, meaning that replicas
that have delivered the same updates eventually reach equivalent states [9]. CRDTs are a class
of replicated data structures aimed to satisfy SEC by construction. They mix conflict resolu-

merged deterministically without any coordination. Early work observed that both state-based
and operation-based CRDTs can be specified by algebraic conditions: in state-based CRDTs, the

(operations) are broadcast in causal order and they have to commute so that replicas that apply
the same set of operations reach the same state [9].

2.2 Formal specification and verification frameworks
Early verification efforts were conducted to provide machine-checked proofs that CRDT designs
actually satisfy SEC. Zeller, Bieniusa and Poetzsch-Heffter developed an Isabelle/HOL framework for specifying and verifying CRDTs [17]. Their framework models an specific number of
replicas. Their model abstracts over payloads, update operations and merge functions, representing each replica’s state and history with associated metadata and version vectors. This
framework enabled the first machine checked convergence proofs for some CRDTs.
While the Isabelle framework is practical, it requires significant manual effort. To reduce this
detail, Nagar and Jagannathan formulated a parameterised verification apporach that obtains
convergence conditions from a given consistency model [8]. Their engine shows that not all
operations must commute to guarantee convergence and produces automated proofs for CRDTs
under various weak consistency policies [8].
Separation logic has been transformed to verify CRDT implementations. Nieto et al. intro-

using Aneris distributed separation logic. These libraries abstract away low-level concurrency
concerns and provide mechanised Coq proofs for a collection of replicated data structures [9].
The approach was later extended to state-based CRDTs because states grow monotonically to

Recent research also uses type systems, domain-specific languages and program synthesis to

and converts verified implementations to mainstream languages [11]. Liang and Feng observed

into SMT logic and searching for suitable semilattice representations [5].

2.3 Byzantine fault tolerance and CRDTs
Kleppmann showed that augmenting CRDT algorithms with signatures, hashes and vector clocks
can tolerate an arbitrary number of Byzantine replicas while preserving strong eventual consistency [4]. More recent work identifies CRDT classes that prevent equivocation; for example,
Jacob, Bayreuther and Hartenstein observed that the Matrix Event Graph stores updates in a
hash-based directed acyclic graph and tolerates equivocation without additional coordination [3].
Such results highlight both the feasibility and the limits of building Byzantine-tolerant CRDTs.
Tschudin proposed a “connectionless” grow-only set CRDT that operates over lossy, reorderable channels without the necessity of any persistent connections between nodes. This CRDT
uses a single message type and incremental updates to spread state, making it suitable for ap-

2.4 Verification in actor and concurrency frameworks
Musser and Varela developed a structured reasoning framework for actor systems using the
Athena proof language. They established abstract theorems about fairness, persistence, message
delivery and progress that hold for any actor system, independently of implementation details
[7]. Their work demonstrates that high-level algebraic reasoning can give general properties such
as guaranteed message delivery and monotonicity of actor states [7]. These results motivate the
use of high-level proof environments when reasoning about replicated data structures.
Formalisation of concurrent languages has also lead to the development of modular semantics
used in many proof frameworks. Bereczky, Horpácsi and Thompson presented a machine-checked
formalisation of concurrent Core Erlang using modular frame stack semantics [1]. They proved
properties such as determinism, confluence and program equivalence [1] providing a foundation
for verifying concurrency properties of actor-based programming languages.
The actor model has also inspired languages for dynamic and open systems. For instance,
SALSA compiles to Java and provides primitives for actor creation, asynchronous messaging,
state updates, continuations and universal naming to coordinate distributed actors [15]. It
emphasises disconected message passing and mobility, repeating with the asynchronous update
and merge semantics of CRDTs and illustrate how actor based languages can support reasoning
about distributed computations.

2.4.1 Program Synthesis and Automated CRDT Generation
Katara (2022) introduced a novel approach to synthesizing CRDTs using verified lifting, allowing developers to generate CRDT implementations automatically from sequential data types.
This synthesis approach leverages lightweight ordering constraints and inductive invariants enabling the automated generation and verification of new CRDTs [5]. This automation addresses

2.4.3 Discussion
From the comparative analysis, it becomes evident that formal verification remains one of the
most significant challenges in CRDT development. Early frameworks like Isabelle/HOL provide
rigorous correctness guarantees but require expert involvement. Automated approaches such as
parameterised verification [8], separation logic libraries [9], type systems [16] and domain-specific
languages [11] lower the burden of proof but they are often tailored to specific models. Recent
efforts to provide abstractions for functional correctness such as ACC [6], and equivocation
tolerance in Byzantine environments [3] suggest that when CRDTs are used as building blocks
in complex systems richer correctness specifications are required.

While many researchers have provided grammars and rules for CRDT operations, fewer have
focused on automating the verification process. Tools like Katara and the verification engine
developed by Nagar and Jagannathan represent important advances in reducing the complexity of designing and verifying CRDTs [5, 8]. Work on Byzantine fault tolerance shows that
many CRDT algorithms can be extended to handle malicious replicas through cryptographic

significant contributions in this regard, but much remains to be explored in ensuring that CRDTs
can function reliably in adverse environments [4].

2.4.4 Conclusion
The research on CRDTs has advanced significantly over the last decade, particularly in defining
the grammar and rules for these data structures and verifying their convergence and consistency.
However, challenges like automating the verification process and extending CRDTs to tolerate
Byzantine faults remain. As distributed systems continue to evolve, the ability to design, implement, and verify CRDTs will be crucial for building scalable, resilient systems.

3 Theoretical Foundations
State-based Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types rely on a simple but powerful mathematical
foundation: join-semilattice theory. A join-semilattice is an algebraic structure in which any two
elements can be combined via a join operation that is commutative, associative and idempotent.
These three properties ensure that replicas merging their states in different orders will converge
to the same result, which is the cornerstone of Strong Eventual Consistency (SEC).
This chapter develops the formal theory underlying our framework. We begin by defining
join-semilattices and explaining how their algebraic properties guarantee convergence. We then
present the abstract CRDT specification consisting of five axioms that, when satisfied, yield
SEC. Finally, we demonstrate how concrete CRDTs such as GCounter, GSet and PN-Counter
instantiate this abstract theory and inherit convergence guarantees through a theory/adapter
proof pattern.
The theory layer serves as the foundation for the framework architecture introduced in Chapter 4. By separating the mathematical model from the operational semantics, we achieve modularity and reuse: once we prove that a data type forms a semilattice, all convergence theorems
follow automatically.

3.1 Semilattices
A join-semilattice is a set equipped with a binary operation satisfying three axioms. Formally:
Definition 3.1 (Join-Semilattice). A join-semilattice is a structure (S, ⊔) where S is a set and
⊔ : S × S → S is a binary operation satisfying:
1. Commutativity: ∀x, y ∈ S. x ⊔ y = y ⊔ x

3 Theoretical Foundations
2. Associativity: ∀x, y, z ∈ S. (x ⊔ y) ⊔ z = x ⊔ (y ⊔ z)
3. Idempotence: ∀x ∈ S. x ⊔ x = x
These three properties, collectively known as the ACI axioms, are fundamental to CRDT convergence. Each property plays a specific role:
• Commutativity ensures that the order in which two states are merged does not affect
the result. If replica A merges with replica B, and replica B merges with replica A, they
obtain the same state.
• Associativity ensures that when merging multiple states, the grouping does not matter.
Replicas can merge states pairwise in any order and reach the same outcome.
• Idempotence ensures that merging a state with itself yields the same state. This property
is crucial for handling duplicate messages or repeated merge operations.
The join operation induces a partial order on S defined by:
x ≤ y ⇐⇒ x ⊔ y = y.
Intuitively, x ≤ y means that y contains at least as much information as x. The join operation
computes the least upper bound of two elements with respect to this order. Figure 3.1 illustrates
a simple join-semilattice as a Hasse diagram, where edges represent the covering relation and the
join of two elements is their least common ancestor.
This partial order interpretation is central to understanding CRDT convergence: updates must
be monotonic, meaning they only move states upward in the lattice. Once a replica has reached
a certain state, subsequent merges can only increase information, never decrease it.

3.2 State-based CRDT model
Building on the semilattice foundation, we define an abstract state-based CRDT as a structure
with five core components and corresponding axioms. This specification captures the essential
requirements for Strong Eventual Consistency without committing to a particular implementation.

12

3.2 State-based CRDT model
Definition 3.2 (Abstract CRDT). A CRDT is a structure (S, V, initial, update, merge) where:
• S is the state domain (elements of the semilattice)
• V is the update domain (operations that modify state)
• initial : () → S returns the bottom element
• update : S × V → S applies an update to a state
• merge : S × S → S is the semilattice join operation
satisfying the five axioms listed below.

Convergence axioms
The abstract specification requires five axioms that together guarantee convergence. For all
states s, s1 , s2 , s3 ∈ S and updates v ∈ V :
A1. Merge Commutativity: merge(s1 , s2 ) = merge(s2 , s1 ).
A2. Merge Associativity: merge(merge(s1 , s2 ), s3 ) = merge(s1 , merge(s2 , s3 )).
A3. Merge Idempotence: merge(s, s) = s.
A4. Initial Identity: merge(initial(), s) = s.
A5. Update Monotonicity: merge(s, update(s, v)) = update(s, v).

Strong Eventual Consistency
Strong Eventual Consistency (SEC) is a correctness criterion for replicated data types introduced
by Shapiro et al. [12]. It consists of two properties:
Definition 3.3 (Strong Eventual Consistency). A replicated data type satisfies SEC if it guarantees:
1. Eventual Delivery: An update delivered at one replica is eventually delivered to all replicas.
2. Convergence: Replicas that have delivered the same set of updates have equivalent states.
Eventual delivery is a liveness property that depends on the underlying network and delivery
mechanism; it is typically assumed rather than proven. Convergence, on the other hand, is a
safety property that can be proven from the CRDT axioms. The key insight is that the semilattice
structure ensures deterministic convergence.

Convergence theorem
We now state the main convergence theorem and show how it follows from the five axioms.
Theorem 3.1 (Strong Eventual Consistency Theorem). Let (S, V, initial, update, merge) be a
CRDT satisfying the five axioms. For any initial state s0 ∈ S and any finite set of updates
U = {v1 , . . . , vn } ⊆ V , all delivery orders produce the same final state after all updates have been
applied and merged.
Proof sketch. Consider two replicas R1 and R2 starting from the same initial state s0 . Suppose
they each receive the updates in U but in different orders. Let σ1 and σ2 be permutations of U
representing the delivery orders at R1 and R2 , respectively.
After delivering all updates, R1 reaches state:
s1 = update(· · · update(update(s0 , σ1 (1)), σ1 (2)) · · · , σ1 (n)),
and R2 reaches state:
s2 = update(· · · update(update(s0 , σ2 (1)), σ2 (2)) · · · , σ2 (n)).
By Axiom 5 (monotonicity), each update moves the state upward: s ≤ update(s, v). Therefore,
both s1 and s2 are upper bounds of s0 and all intermediate states.
Now suppose the replicas exchange their states and merge them. By Axiom 1 (commutativity):
merge(s1 , s2 ) = merge(s2 , s1 ).
By repeated application of Axiom 2 (associativity) and Axiom 3 (idempotence), this merge
can be rewritten as the join of all individual updates in any order. Since the join operation is
commutative and associative, the final state is independent of the delivery order.
More formally, the final merged state is:
merge(s1 , s2 ) = s0 ⊔ update(s0 , v1 ) ⊔ · · · ⊔ update(s0 , vn ),
which is the same regardless of the permutations σ1 and σ2 .
This theorem establishes that the five axioms are sufficient for convergence. It also reveals
why each axiom is necessary:

• Commutativity ensures that merging s1 with s2 yields the same result as merging s2
with s1 , so replicas converge regardless of which replica merges first.
• Associativity ensures that the grouping of merges does not matter, allowing replicas to
merge states incrementally without affecting the outcome.
• Idempotence ensures that duplicate deliveries or redundant merges do not change the
final state.
• Identity ensures that the initial state does not interfere with subsequent merges.
• Monotonicity ensures that updates only increase information, preventing states from
moving backward in the lattice.

Shapiro et al. formalized this state-based model and proved that if the merge operation is
commutative, associative, and idempotent and the update function is monotonic, then any two
replicas that apply the same set of updates (possibly in different orders) will eventually reach
an identical state [12]. This is exactly the Strong Eventual Consistency result we have derived
above.

4 Framework Architecture
4.1 Overview of the Layered Architecture
The verification framework is organized as a stack of four logical layers: the Theory layer at the
foundation, the Payload layer above it, the Backbone layer next, and concrete CRDT implementations at the top. Each layer builds upon the abstractions of the layer below it, maintaining
a clear separation of concerns. At the base is the Theory layer, which provides the mathematical foundation (join-semilattice laws and abstract CRDT properties). Above it, the Payload
layer realises generic state-based CRDT semantics (local state and merge operations). Next, the
Backbone layer adds replication mechanics, including vector clocks and causal message delivery,
to support operation-based CRDTs. Finally, the top layer consists of concrete CRDT implementations, which plug into the framework via lightweight adapters. This layered design isolates
different aspects of the system: algebraic correctness, state semantics, replication protocol and
application-specific logic are all handled in separate modules. Such modularity makes the framework extensible and eases reasoning, since each layer can be specified and verified in isolation
and then composed to obtain full end-to-end guarantees.
The layers interact in a disciplined way. The theory layer specifies the convergence requirements that any CRDT must satisfy; the payload and backbone layers implement a generic runtime
that will enforce these requirements, provided the CRDT’s state and update functions meet the
theory’s axioms. Concrete CRDTs are integrated last, by instantiating the abstract definitions
with their own state sets and operations. Because of the strict separation, changes in one layer
(for example, using a different network protocol in the backbone, or adding a new CRDT type)
do not require re-proving properties of the other layers. In the remainder of this chapter, we detail each layer of the architecture. Section 4.2 covers the foundational theory module, Section 4.3

4 Framework Architecture
describes the payload semantics, Section 4.5 discusses the backbone for causal consistency, and
Section 4.6 explains how concrete CRDT implementations are connected via adapters.

4.2 Theory Layer: SemilatticeTheory and Abstract CRDT
At the foundation of the framework is a formal semilattice theory that captures the algebraic
conditions for convergence. This theory layer (developed in Chapter 3) defines an abstract
CRDT specification through a set of five axioms. These axioms are: (i) commutativity, (ii)
associativity, and (iii) idempotence of the merge operator (the three properties making the merge
a join-semilattice operation), (iv) existence of an identity element (an initial state that acts as
a neutral element for merge), and (v) monotonicity of updates (every update must only increase
the state according to the semilattice order). When a data type’s state and update functions
satisfy these properties, it guarantees that all replicas will reach the same state as soon as they
have delivered the same set of updates, regardless of order. In other words, the axioms imply the
Strong Eventual Consistency (SEC) property of CRDTs, as established by Shapiro et al. [12].
The role of this layer is critical: it provides a reusable mathematical foundation that all CRDT
instances share. Key convergence theorems (for example, that merge operations commute to
a common outcome) are proven once at this abstract level. Later in Chapter 5, we will see
that each concrete CRDT can inherit these theorems by showing that its particular state set
and operations instantiate the Theory layer’s axioms. This approach follows a theory/adapter
pattern: the heavy lifting is done in the general theory, and each CRDT needs only to provide
a small proof (via an adapter) that the theory’s conditions hold in its case. By separating the
mathematical model from any operational details, the Theory layer enables a high degree of proof
reuse and confidence: as soon as a new CRDT is proven to form a semilattice with monotonic
updates, all the convergence results from Chapter 3 immediately apply to it.
It is worth noting that the foundation also includes some general-purpose utilities that support
the upper layers. For example, the framework provides library modules for finite maps (to
represent mappings from replica identifiers to states or timestamps), multisets (to represent
network message buffers), and a formalisation of vector clocks (finitely supported maps from
replica IDs to counters). These components, while not part of the core CRDT theory, form a

useful substrate for building the payload and backbone layers.

4.3 Payload Layer: State-Based Semantics
The Payload layer implements the core semantics of state-based CRDTs on top of the abstract
theory. This layer is realised by the PayloadFramework module, which defines how CRDT
states are maintained and how they evolve through message passing. Importantly, the payload
framework remains parametric over the actual CRDT state and update types: it assumes nothing
about the concrete data structure beyond the semilattice properties ensured by the Theory layer.
Instead, it provides a generic execution model that any semilattice-based CRDT can plug into.

4.3.1 Mathematical Model
Let (S, ⊔) be a join-semilattice: a set S equipped with a binary operation ⊔ : S × S → S
that is commutative, associative and idempotent. It induces a partial order ≤ on S by the rule
x ≤ y ⇐⇒ x⊔y = y. This makes (S, ≤) a partially ordered set with least upper bounds: for any
x, y ∈ S, x ⊔ y is the least element z such that x ≤ z and y ≤ z. A CRDT’s state lives in such a
semilattice. The merge of two replica states corresponds to ⊔; it must therefore be commutative,
associative and idempotent. Updates are modelled by a function apply : S × U → S, where U
is the set of update operations. To guarantee convergence, updates must be monotonic: for all
s ∈ S and u ∈ U , we require s ≤ apply(s, u) [12]. This ensures that applying the same update
multiple times does not revert the state or break convergence.

4.5.2 Composition of State and Causality
The Backbone layer leverages the Theory layer by observing that vector clocks form a semilattice
in their own right. The backbone combines the clock lattice with the payload’s state lattice. We
effectively work in the product lattice S × V C: an element of the product is a pair (s, v) of a
payload state and a vector clock. The join is defined as:
(s1 , v1 ) ⊔ (s2 , v2 ) = (s1 ⊔S s2 , v1 ⊔V C v2 ),
that is, the payload states are joined by their ⊔ and the clocks by pointwise maximum. This
product construction is again a join-semilattice (it inherits ACI properties from each component). As a result, the entire backbone configuration space still satisfies the convergence axioms.
Despite the added complexity of tracking clocks, the backbone layer’s operations preserve the
fundamental algebraic structure needed for SEC. This modular composition allows us to prove
convergence piecewise: the Backbone layer correctly maintains causal delivery, and because the
combined state+clock forms a semilattice, the convergence theorems from Chapter 3 still apply
to the system as a whole.

4.6 CRDT Adapters and Instantiation
The final layer of the architecture is where concrete CRDT implementations come into play.
Rather than building each CRDT from scratch, the framework uses an adapter pattern to instantiate the generic payload/backbone machinery with the details of a particular CRDT. A
CRDT adapter is a thin module that connects a CRDT’s native state and operations to the
interfaces expected by the Payload (and optionally Backbone) framework.
To create a new CRDT within our framework, one follows a systematic process:

29

4 Framework Architecture
1. Define the state and update types for the CRDT. For example, for a Grow-Only Counter
(GCounter), the state might be a natural number (or a map of replica IDs to numbers),
and the update is an increment operation.
2. Provide the merge and apply functions. In the GCounter, the merge operation is typically
the pointwise maximum of two counters. The update (apply) function for an increment is
just addition on the local replica’s component. Similarly, for a Grow-Only Set (GSet), the
merge is set union and the update is an element insertion. Each CRDT’s design dictates
these two key operations.
3. Prove that the five framework axioms hold. The adapter must ensure that the provided
merge is commutative, associative, idempotent, has an identity element and that the update
is monotonic with respect to the merge. In practice, these proofs are straightforward
because they boil down to simple algebraic laws of the chosen data type. For instance, for
GCounter we reduce all required properties to facts about max over N. These simple laws
are usually either obvious or well-documented properties of the data structure. The heavy
lifting has already been done in the Theory layer: the adapter’s job is only to verify that
the CRDT at hand conforms to that theory.
4. Instantiate the framework modules. Once the CRDT’s operations are validated against the
axioms, we create a concrete instance of PayloadOps (and if using the backbone, an instance
of the combined backbone payload operations that includes clock management). This
involves writing a small adapter module that defines the CRDT’s specific merge, apply,
initial state and so on, and then uses the generic PayloadFramework and CRDTBackbone
to obtain the executable specification.
This adapter approach has several benefits. First, it dramatically reduces verification effort.
All the core theorems about convergence, idempotence of merges and similar properties have
already been proven in the abstract framework. A new CRDT does not require re-proving those
from scratch; one only checks the axioms for that CRDT’s specific operations. The proofs for
each adapter are typically concise, as they often reuse library lemmas. Once those checks are
done, the framework guarantees the CRDT will behave correctly in a replicated setting.

30

4.6 CRDT Adapters and Instantiation
Second, the adapter pattern enforces a consistent structure across all CRDT implementations.
Every CRDT in our framework has a similar shape of specification: a state type, a merge (join)
operation, an update function and an initial state. This makes it easy to add new CRDTs by
analogy. For instance, implementing a Positive-Negative Counter (PN-Counter) can be done by
composing two GCounter adapters and showing that the pair of naturals with pairwise maximum
forms a valid semilattice (which it does, as a direct product of two max-semilattices). The framework naturally supports such product constructions: if the components satisfy the semilattice
axioms, so does their combination.
Finally, the adapters serve as a bridge from abstract theory to executable code. They connect
the mathematical model to concrete operations that can run. Because our framework is built
in the Athena proof assistant, these specifications are not only proven correct but can also be
executed (for simulation or testing). The same adapter that we prove correct is used to generate
a running instance of the CRDT in a demo or test harness. This tight feedback loop helps ensure
that the verified model truly corresponds to an implementable artefact.
In Chapter 5, we showcase six verified CRDTs built with this framework (GCounter, GSet, PNCounter, Two-Phase Set, OR-Set and LWW-Register). Each is defined by a small adapter module
that plugs into the Payload layer (for state-based CRDTs) or the combined Payload+Backbone
layers (for those that need causal delivery). The result is that each of these CRDTs comes
with strong convergence guarantees: since their adapters satisfy the Theory layer axioms, the
Payload/Backbone execution will ensure SEC and other correctness properties proven in the base
framework. This demonstrates the power of the theory/adapter approach in achieving modular
verification: we verify generic convergence once, and then validate each new CRDT via a brief
conformance proof.

5.4 The Adapter Pattern in Practice
A critical insight of our framework is the adapter pattern: each CRDT is realized by a thin
adapter module that plugs concrete operations into the generic Payload or Backbone framework.
This section details how adapters work and why they enable dramatic reduction in verification
effort.

5.4.1 Adapter Structure
Each CRDT adapter follows a consistent template:
1. Specification module (e.g., GCounterSpec): Defines the state type, operations, and basic
behavioral properties.
2. Theory instantiation: Proves that the merge operation satisfies the five framework axioms (commutativity, associativity, idempotence, identity, monotonicity).

40

5.4 The Adapter Pattern in Practice
3. Framework instantiation: Provides concrete implementations of PayloadOps (or BBPayloadOps
for causal CRDTs) that plug the CRDT into the framework.
4. Executable adapter: Uses the framework’s configuration machinery to create runnable
instances for testing and demonstration.
Example: GCounter Adapter.

The GCounter adapter (168 lines) defines:

• State type: Ide → N (map from replica IDs to counters)
• Operations:
– gc-merge(s1 , s2 ) := λr. max(s1 (r), s2 (r))
– gc-apply(s, increment(δ)) := s + δ
– gc-initial := zero
• Proof instantiation: Proves merge is commutative, associative, idempotent via reduction
to max over N (which are library lemmas already proven in framework/nat-max)
• Framework integration: Registers the adapter with PayloadFramework, inheriting all
convergence theorems
The beauty of this approach is that the heavy lifting has already been done. The axiom
proofs are brief because: - Commutativity: max on naturals is commutative (library lemma
N.Max.commutative) - Associativity: max is associative (library lemma N.Max.associative)
- Idempotence: max(x, x) = x (library lemma N.Max.idempotent) - Identity: max(0, x) = x
(library lemma N.Max.identity) - Monotonicity: increment is monotonic (addition preserves
≤ on N)
Once these five proofs are discharged, the framework’s convergence theorem applies directly:
any two replicas that deliver the same increments, in any order, reach identical states.

5.4.2 Proof Reuse and Composition
Simple adapters (GCounter, GSet). GCounter’s convergence proof is a direct instantiation of
the framework’s generic semilattice convergence theorem. Similarly, GSet’s convergence follows
from the fact that set union is a join-semilattice.

Product semilattices (PN-Counter, Two-Phase Set). One of the most elegant aspects is that
products of semilattices are themselves semilattices. PN-Counter is the product (Ide →
N) × (Ide → N), with componentwise operations. Because both components satisfy the ACI
axioms, so does the product. The PN-Counter adapter (197 lines) primarily reuses GCounter’s
proofs for the two components; the only new work is verifying that decrement is also monotonic.
This demonstrates horizontal proof reuse: multiple CRDTs leverage the same base theory.

Causal CRDTs (OR-Set, LWW-Register).

Causal CRDTs are more involved because they use

the Backbone framework, which adds vector clock management. However, the principle remains:

the visibility predicate respects causality. The Backbone framework then guarantees that causal
delivery combined with merge convergence yields overall correctness.

7.2 Lessons Learned
- Semilattice abstraction pays off : Making ACI explicit simplifies convergence arguments
and clarifies invariants. - Theory/adapter pattern is powerful: Reduces from O(n2 ) perCRDT proofs to O(n) adapters; product constructions (e.g., PN, 2P) come almost for free. Athena trade-offs: High signal-to-noise in equational proofs and easy execution, at the cost
of less tactic automation than Isabelle/Coq. - Figures and small snippets help: Lattices,
timelines, and short code signatures communicate ideas better than long listings.

7.3 Future Directions
- Delta-CRDTs: Add delta-join and anti-entropy schemas; reuse semilattice laws with payloadlevel deltas. - Operation-based CRDTs with causal broadcast: Integrate a verified causal
broadcast and delivery guards into the backbone. - Automated adapter synthesis: Derive
adapter skeletons from type signatures and target base theories (Max/Union/Product). - Integration with runtime systems: Link the executable semantics to message middleware (actors
or Raft-like transports) for system-level validation.

8 Conclusions and Future Work
This thesis developed a modular framework for verifying replicated data types in distributed
systems. The central challenge addressed is ensuring strong eventual consistency (SEC) in the
presence of concurrency and asynchronous message delivery. By combining algebraic models
with causal metadata, the system guarantees convergence without central coordination.
We began from first principles, formalising the semilattice model that underpins CRDT convergence. This abstraction provides the minimal requirements—commutativity, associativity,
idempotence, and monotonicity—under which concurrent state updates can be merged deterministically. Chapter 3 captured these axioms as reusable Athena theories, which are later
instantiated by concrete CRDT designs.
From this foundation, the framework provides two complementary layers: the Payload layer
captures CRDT update semantics over a semilattice, while the Backbone layer extends this
with vector clocks and message delivery, enforcing causal order (both detailed in Chapter 4).
The Payload layer ensures algebraic convergence; the Backbone guarantees that the delivery
mechanism respects causality and applies each update at most once.
Six CRDTs were implemented using this framework (Chapter 5). Each instantiates the abstract
semantics, providing concrete merge and update operations, and discharging the required axioms.
Formal proofs were constructed in Athena, demonstrating correctness by construction: once a
CRDT conforms to the theory interface, convergence theorems follow automatically.
The framework was validated through both theoretical proofs and executable demonstrations
(Chapter 6). Each layer was shown to satisfy its intended properties: Payload implements the
semilattice model; Backbone preserves causal consistency; and together they yield SEC. All artifacts are open-source and publicly available at https://github.com/FLAGlab/Athena-CRDTs.
